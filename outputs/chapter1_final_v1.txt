CHAPTER I: THE CANOE BUILDER

Dick stood at the edge of the coral ledge, his gaze scanning the southern horizon. The thunderous breakers of the outer sea crashed behind him, and the spindrift scattered on the wind. Before him, the lagoon of Karolin stretched out, a seemingly endless expanse of calm, blue water, dotted with fishing gulls.

The tranquility of the ocean was a far cry from the turbulent seas they had faced just yesterday. Karolin's lagoon was a sea in itself, with waves crashing against the shore during heavy winds and a lake of turquoise calmness on lighter days. And it was his – Dick's – domain. He had claimed the land, and with it, the tribe that inhabited it.

The people of Karolin, women, children, and youths, bustled along the beach, their activities illuminated by the blazing sun. They fished with nets, played games, or worked on the paraka patches, all under the watchful eye of their new leader, Dick. The canoes drawn up on the sand and the empty houses where the war canoes once rested on their rollers were all his.

As Dick gazed out at the lagoon, his brow furrowed, and he turned his back on the sea. He faced the outer beach, the northern sea, and the invisible island of Palm Tree, which lay beyond the horizon. Tari, the son of Le Taioi, the net maker, sat on the coral nearby, watching Dick with curiosity-filled eyes.

Tari knew little of life, but he knew that all the men of Karolin who had gone to war had left the women and children defenseless and without a leader. Then, yesterday, Dick had arrived, sent by the gods, according to the women, to be their chief and ruler.

Little Tari put the question directly to Dick, his eyes sparkling with curiosity: "Taori, who are you?" Dick's gaze lingered on the sea, lost in thought, before he turned to Tari. He couldn't answer, and Tari knew that the strange new person wouldn't understand the story he might have told.

Dick's mind began to stir, and he saw the schooner that had brought the Melanesian devils to Palm Tree, and he saw his escape with Katafa in the old dinghy. He recalled their landing on this beach, yesterday, and the women and children swarming around him, considering him a god-sent leader.

As he stood there, Dick's thoughts turned to the three old men he had sent to fetch – Aioma, Palia, and Tafata – expert canoe builders. He had also noticed that nearly a hundred youths were growing old enough for war, but his mind was consumed by his passion for Katafa, the person who had become his everything.

Katafa's hand fell on his shoulder, and he turned to face her. Her dark hair had escaped from its elastic vine and blew back on the breeze like an eagle's feather. Her eyes, luminous and dark, were fixed on the point where he had been gazing, the due-north sea line.

"Look!" she whispered, her voice barely audible.

Palm Tree, the island they had escaped from, began to materialize on the horizon, a mysterious stain low down in the southern sky. As they stood there, frozen in time, they saw the island come alive, beautiful as a dream, yet vivid and diaphanous.

For a moment, they were transported back to the nightmare that had driven them to escape. They saw the Melanesians, their forms twisted and monstrous, and the wind that had filled the sail as they raced for safety. They remembered the yells of the demons and the schooner they had brought in, only to burn.

As they gazed upon the loveliness of Palm Tree, Dick's eyes grew dark with hate. They had threatened him, but more importantly, they had threatened Katafa. Some day, they would come, and he knew it. The vision of Palm Tree seemed to echo through his mind, a warning and a threat.

The canoe builders arrived, and Dick and Katafa walked down to the inner beach to meet them. As they approached, Dick's thoughts turned darker, his mind consumed by the foundations of history – man's instinct for war, rapine, and destruction.
