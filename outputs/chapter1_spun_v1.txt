**The Gates of Morning**

Chapter 1: The Canoe Builder

Dick stood on the coral ledge, gazing out at the endless blue of the lagoon, his eyes tracing the horizon where the sea and sky merged. Behind him, the thunderous breakers of the outer sea crashed against the shore, sending spindrift scattering on the wind. The lagoon of Karolin stretched out before him, a tranquil sea in itself, surrounded by a 40-mile ring of coral.

As he scanned the beach, he saw the women, children, and youths of the tribe going about their daily activities – fishing, playing, and working on the paraka patches. They were his people, and this was his land. His were the canoes drawn up on the sand, and the empty houses where the war canoes had once rested on their rollers.

But as he turned his back on the lagoon and faced the northern sea, his brow furrowed with concern. Beyond the sea line, the island of Palm Tree lay hidden, its beauty as a dream, yet swarming with devils. Little Tari, the son of Le Taioi, the net maker, watched Dick from the coral, sensing the unease emanating from him.

"I am Taori," Dick said, turning to the child. "Why do you ask?"

Tari's innocent mind had already wandered off, attracted by the commotion of some children hauling in a net. "I ask as I breathe," he said, "but no big folk will ever answer the questions of Tari."

As Dick gazed out at the north, memories began to resurface. He saw the schooner that had burned on Palm Tree, and the escape in the old dinghy with Katafa, the girl who had been blown away from Karolin years ago. He saw their landing on this beach, yesterday, and the women and children swarming around him, considering him sent by the gods to be their chief and leader.

But his thoughts were interrupted by the memory of the men from whom he had escaped, staining the beauty of the sea and sky. There was no immediate danger, but he knew they would build canoes and come to Karolin. He had sent a canoe across the lagoon to fetch the expert canoe builders, Aioma, Palia, and Tafata, and his eyes had brightened at the sight of nearly a hundred youths ripening to war age.

As he stood there, Katafa's hand fell on his shoulder, and he turned to face her. Her dark hair had escaped its elastic vine binding, blowing back on the breeze like an eagle's feather. Her eyes, luminous and dark, were fixed on the north.

"Look," she said, and Dick followed her gaze to the point where Palm Tree was beginning to reappear on the horizon, a mysterious stain low down in the southern sky.

Their minds, untutored and uninformed, knew nothing of mirage, but they saw the island rising before them, beautiful as a dream. They felt again the wind filling the sail, the dinghy racing for safety, and the yells of the Melanesians. And there, before them lay the scene of the Tragedy, a lovely picture that showed nothing of the demons that still inhabited it.

As they gazed on this loveliness, Dick's nostrils expanded, and his eyes grew dark with hate. They had threatened him, that was nothing; they had threatened Katafa, that was everything. And they still threatened her.

Some day, they would come. The vision of Palm Tree repeated what instinct told him – they would build canoes, see the lagoon light, and come. They had no women, those men, and here were women, and instinct whispered to him that just as he had been drawn to Katafa, so would these men be drawn to the women of Karolin.

Ah, if only he had known, danger lay not only to the north, but wherever greed or desire or hatred might roam on that azure sea, not only amongst savages, but the wolves of civilization.

As the vision of Palm Tree faded, Katafa pointed across the lagoon to a canoe approaching from the southern beach. It was the canoe Dick had sent for the expert builders, and they made their way down to the white sand to meet it.
