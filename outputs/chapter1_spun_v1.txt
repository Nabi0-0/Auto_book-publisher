CHAPTER I: THE CANOE BUILDER

Dick stood on a coral ledge, his eyes fixed on the southern horizon. Behind him, the waves of the outer sea crashed against the shore, and the sea spray danced on the wind. Before him, the lagoon of Karolin stretched out, a calm and endless expanse of blue water, dotted with fishing birds. It was his, he who had arrived just yesterday, and it was his people – the women, children, and youths busily working along the beach, playing, or tending to their paraka patches.

As he scanned the beach, his brow furrowed, and he turned his back to the lagoon, facing the breakers on the outer beach and the northern sea. Beyond the horizon, invisible, lay Palm Tree, an island of beauty and danger, swarming with devils. Little Tari, the son of Le Taioi, the net maker, gazed up at him from the coral rocks. Tari knew little about life, but he knew that all the men of Karolin who had gone to war had left the women and children, including himself, defenseless and leaderless.

Then, yesterday, from the northern sea, in a strange boat, with Katafa, the girl who had been swept away from this shore years ago, had come a new figure, sent by the gods, the women said, to be their chief and ruler.

The child knew nothing about the gods, nor did he care, alone now with this new person and out of earshot of his mother, he asked the question direct and simple, "Taori, who are you?" (é kamina tai)

Dick couldn't answer, nor would the child have understood the strange words of his story. But little did he know, his life was about to change forever.

He looked down at the questioning child, "I am Taori, Tari tatu, why do you ask?"

"I don't know," replied the child, "I ask as I breathe, but no big folk – madyana – will ever answer the questions of Tari – Ai, the fish!" His agile mind had already moved on, drawn by the cries of some children hauling in a net, and he rose and trotted away.

Dick's gaze returned to the north, and the question stirred his mind. He saw again the schooner that had arrived at Palm Tree, only to be burned by the hands of the Melanesian men, and he saw again Katafa and himself as they made their escape in the old dinghy. He saw their landing on this beach, yesterday, and the women and children swarming around him, considering him sent by the gods to be their chief and leader.

As he gazed towards the north, the memory of the men who had driven them away stained the beauty of the sea and sky. There was no immediate danger, but he knew they would come, and he would have to prepare.

Dick was a man with a past shrouded in mist, but he knew he could think forward, imagine, and plan. That's why he had sent a canoe that morning to fetch three old canoe builders, and why his eyes had brightened at the sight of nearly a hundred youths ripening to war age.

But beneath it all, lighting his mind, raising his daring to eagle heights, lay his passion for Katafa, his other self, more dear to him than life itself. And so, the thought of war canoes, and the danger that lay beyond the horizon, weighed heavily on his mind.

A hand fell on his shoulder, and he turned to face Katafa, her dark hair blowing back like an eagle's feather, her eyes fixed on the sea line. "Look!" she said.

Palm Tree, though far behind the sea line, began to appear through the mirage, a beautiful, diaphanous vision. They saw again the savages on the beach, the wind in the dinghy's sail, and the yells of the Melanesians. And as they gazed, Dick's nostrils expanded, and his eyes grew dark with hate. They had threatened him, and they had threatened Katafa, and they still threatened her.

Some day, they would come, and Dick knew it. They would build canoes and see the lagoon light in the sky, and they would come. Ah, if he had known, danger lay not only to the north, but wherever greed or desire or hatred might roam on that azure sea, not only amongst savages, but the wolves of civilization.

But for now, he focused on the canoe approaching from the southern beach, bearing the canoe builders he had sent for. Leaving the coral, they made their way down to the white sand of the inner beach to meet it.
