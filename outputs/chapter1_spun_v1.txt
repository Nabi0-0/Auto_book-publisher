The Gates of Morning

Chapter 1: The Canoe Builder

Dick stood on the coral ledge, gazing out at the endless expanse of the lagoon. The outer sea thundered behind him, and the fishing gulls soared overhead, their cries carried away by the wind. Before him, the lagoon stretched like a lake, its crystal waters reflecting the vibrant blue of the sky. This was Karolin, his new home, where he had arrived only yesterday.

As he scanned the beach, he noticed the tribe busy with their daily routines: fishing, playing, or working on the paraka patches. His eyes landed on the canoes drawn up on the sand, and the empty houses where the war canoes had once rested on their rollers. All of it was his now.

Tari, the young boy of Le Taioi, the net maker, sat on the coral nearby, watching Dick with curiosity. The child was innocent of the world and its dangers, unaware of the wars that had taken the men of Karolin, leaving the women and children defenseless. Yesterday, Dick had arrived, sent by the gods, according to the women, to be their chief and leader.

Little Tari looked up at Dick and asked, "Taori, who are you?"

Dick hesitated, unsure how to explain his past. He had grown up on Marua, an island beyond the reach of the world, with an old sailor named Kearney. They had lived a simple life, fishing and playing, until a great ship appeared, bringing with it the Melanesian savages who had destroyed the island and killed Kearney. Dick and Katafa, the girl who had been blown away from Karolin, had escaped in a dinghy, taking with them everything they loved, including the little ships Kearney had made.

But Dick couldn't bring himself to tell Tari the whole story. It was too complicated, and the memories were still too raw. Instead, he replied, "I am Taori, Tari, why do you ask?"

Tari's response was simple and direct: "I ask as I breathe, but no big folk will ever answer the questions of Tari."

Dick turned his gaze back to the north, lost in thought. He saw the schooner that had burned on Palm Tree, and the Melanesian hands that had taken possession of the island. He saw Katafa and himself making their escape, and the landing on this beach, yesterday. The question the child had asked had stirred his mind, and he couldn't shake the feeling that the men from Palm Tree would return.

As he stood there, a hand fell upon his shoulder, and he turned to see Katafa, her dark hair blowing in the breeze, her eyes fixed on the distant sea line. "Look!" she exclaimed.

Palm Tree, the island of their past, was visible once more, rising from the horizon like a mirage. Dick and Katafa stood there, their minds untutored and unaware of the world beyond the water that ringed the two islands. They saw the savages on the beach, the wind in the sails, and the yells of the Melanesians. And they felt again the threat of war, the danger that lurked beyond the horizon.

As they gazed upon the island, Dick's eyes grew dark with hate. They had threatened him, and they had threatened Katafa. They still threatened her. He knew that someday they would come, building canoes and seeking the women of Karolin. He felt the instinct of war, rapine, and destruction, the foundation of all history.

The vision of Palm Tree faded, and the canoe approaching from the southern beach came into view. It was the canoe Dick had sent for the canoe builders, and he and Katafa made their way to the inner beach to meet it. The fate of Karolin and its people hung in the balance, as Dick and Katafa stood on the threshold of a new era, unaware of the dangers that lay ahead.
